Entering a Whole New World

Chapter Three

Bait

— Goh's Perspective —

We gave him a two-minute head start, which felt like both far too long and nowhere near enough.

Satoshi checked the pistol in his hands for the third time, turning the silencer screwed onto the end over and over like it might explain itself if he looked at it long enough. Neither of us had ever fired a gun before tonight. I didn't say that out loud. He probably already knew. Between the two of us we had the pistol, a katana I had no idea how to swing properly, and a baseball bat Satoshi had picked purely, I suspected, because it felt familiar in his hands.

"You good?" he asked me.

"No," I said. "But I don't think 'good' is on the menu tonight."

He almost smiled at that. Almost.

Two minutes ticked by. We moved.

— Max's Perspective —

Two pistols, a katana, the submachine gun, three grenades, and the walkie-talkie — all of it crammed into the bag now slung across my back. The Bullet's engine caught on the second kick, loud enough that I felt it in my teeth before I really heard it. Knife clamped between my teeth, blade out, I drew the edge across my own palm and held the cut up into the open air.

The smell hit the street before I did.

Heads turned. All of them, all at once, like the whole horde had been one animal wearing a hundred different bodies. I gunned the engine and went the other way, screaming every insult I could think of, firing the submachine gun in short bursts at nothing in particular, because accuracy wasn't the point — noise was.

"Come and get me, then!" I shouted, voice already cracking. "Fuckers! That's it — keep looking at me, not them!"

It worked. It worked almost too well.

Here was the part I hadn't told them. The antidote wasn't really an antidote — not the clean kind. The moment that fungus got into my blood, my own white blood cells went to war with it, and with me a little, in the process. Small, violent flare-ups, over and over, like my body was trying to burn the invader out from the inside. My temperature would spike. If it spiked far enough, it would scramble my brain before the fungus ever got the chance to take the wheel.

It wasn't a cure. It was a race to see which thing finished me first — and "doesn't become a zombie" was the only prize on offer either way. It sounded insane, even to me. But the logic underneath it actually held up. Picture five people in a sealed room with one zombie loose, none of them protected. One bite, and it's four against a smaller, more frightened group. Bite by bite, it collapses — by the end, maybe nobody walks out. Now picture the same five people, all of them carrying what was in my blood. One bite still costs a life. But it stops there. The fungus runs out of road to travel. Four people walk out instead of zero.

That was the plan. That was also, if I was honest, a very pretty way of saying I might not be one of the four.

I didn't want to die. I just didn't have a better idea.

I didn't see the one that got tangled in my front wheel until the bike was already in the air, and so was I. Ninety kilometers an hour does not care about your plans. For one full second, hanging there with the street rushing up to meet me, I was completely certain this was how it ended.

Then I landed. And nothing broke.

Nothing. Not a bruise I could feel, not one complaint from any part of my body, even though I'd dropped a good ten feet straight onto pavement. I lay there for half a heartbeat, blinking at the sky, waiting for the pain to catch up with the rest of me.

It never did.

I didn't have time to ask why. I just ran.

And running turned out to be its own small miracle, because I was fast — not adrenaline-fast, motorcycle-fast, like something underneath my skin had been waiting its whole life for permission to switch on, and tonight somebody had finally flipped it.

They came from everywhere after that. Not in lines, not from one direction — pouring out of every side street at once, like the whole city had tipped sideways and they were only doing what gravity told them to. I didn't have a destination anymore. I had a building, two pistols, and an idea so stupid I nearly laughed out loud in the middle of running for my life.

I jammed both pistols into the gap between a window frame and its column, grip-first, and started climbing like they were a pair of ice axes.

It was, very specifically, exactly like Getting Over It — that brutal little game where the entire point is flinging yourself up a cliff with a hammer and watching your own one bad swing send you sliding right back down to where you started — except the cliff was twelve stories of glass and concrete, the hammer was two handguns, and falling wasn't really an option, because the ground underneath me had teeth.

It took thirty-seven minutes. My arms stopped feeling like arms somewhere around minute twenty and started feeling like two lengths of rope someone else was operating badly. But I made it. Whatever had switched on in me when I hit the pavement and didn't break was apparently still switched on, because by every rule I used to know, that climb should have been impossible. I didn't have time to be grateful. I just used it.

At the top, I dropped onto the ledge, used my bag as a pillow, and discovered I was completely out of ammunition.

Below me, the zombies were doing something I didn't have a good word for — climbing over each other, body on body, building themselves into an ugly, writhing tower reaching up toward the edge I was sitting on. It wasn't fast. It also wasn't going to stop.

I had exactly one grenade left.

I also had half a chocolate bar in my bag, lifted from the office canteen a few floors below the meeting room, and in a decision that probably says something about my priorities, I ate the chocolate first. Then I dropped the grenade.

It bounced once off a head near the top of the pile and went off, taking a meter or two of height off the climbing zombies and absolutely nothing off their numbers. Which — fine. It had been worth a try.

I knew my limits. A horde the size of the one currently rebuilding its little pyramid below me — and it felt, in that moment, like millions, even though it almost certainly wasn't — was not something I could fight my way through. So I didn't try. I pulled out my phone, screen cracked but alive, plugged in the one earbud that still worked, and put on music, because apparently that was where my brain went when it finally ran out of better ideas.

The walkie-talkie crackled maybe ten minutes later.

"Hey, whe—" Goh started.

"Hey. Where are you. We made it — the lift, all of it, it worked," Satoshi cut in, talking fast.

They'd made it. Of course they had. And they were still waiting on me, even though I'd told them not to, even though every reasonable plan said they should already be long gone.

I told them the truth, because lying felt like it would cost more energy than I had left to spend. "I'm not going to make it to you. Three to five kilometers, and there's a wall of them between here and there that isn't exactly thinning out."

The horde below me was twelve, maybe fifteen meters from the base of the building and closing. I watched the gap shrink while I said it.

And underneath the calm voice I was using on the radio, I was so much more scared than I let on. I didn't want to die. I'd never wanted that — not once, not even in the version of tonight where I told myself I'd already accepted it. My thoughts kept looping back to the same useless place: this is it, this is really it, I don't want this to be it—

"You can make it," Satoshi said. "Just don't give up."

Something in my chest unknotted. Not all the way. Enough.

If I was going to die tonight, I decided, I'd at least die moving.

I pulled up the photo of the map, found my building, found theirs, and did the math. "I'll be there in twenty-seven minutes," I said. "Don't call me, don't make a sound — not unless thirty-five minutes pass and I still haven't shown up. If that happens, it means I didn't make it."

"There's a big collapsible door on the lift," Goh said, after a pause that lasted one second too long. "You'll understand when you get here."

I clipped the walkie-talkie back onto my bag, looked down at the climbing, writhing mass of everything in this city that wanted to kill me, and jumped.
