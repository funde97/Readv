Story Title Two

Chapter One

First Light

The fight started over something small. It always does.

Sai couldn't even remember the exact words anymore — not twenty minutes after it happened. What he remembered was the heat of it. The way his voice cracked when he tried to sound firm. The way his mother's eyes went sharp and cold, the way his father's jaw tightened like a locked gate.

And then — silence. The worst kind of silence. The kind that fills a room after everything has already been said. Sai turned and walked straight to his bedroom. He didn't slam the door. Slamming felt too dramatic, too childish. Instead, he shut it with a slow, deliberate click — which somehow felt worse. He turned the lock. Sat down on the edge of his bed. Pressed his palms against his knees and stared at the floor.

Outside, he could hear the muffled sounds of the house continuing without him. His mother in the kitchen. The low murmur of the television. Life going on. He exhaled slowly and fell back onto the mattress, arms spread wide, staring at the ceiling.

He was nineteen. Second year at one of the country's top engineering universities, studying Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning. He was supposed to be smart. And maybe he was — but right now, lying on his back in his childhood bedroom with anger burning quietly in his chest, he felt anything but.

He picked up his phone. Set it down. Picked it up again.

The clock on the screen read 6:31 PM.

He opened Snapchat.

He scrolled past other names and stopped at one.

Zara.

He stared at her name for a moment longer than he needed to. Then he typed:

"Hey. You free?"

He put the phone face-down on the mattress and waited.

Zara, she had been in his middle school. She sat two rows to his left in their eighth-grade science class, and the first time she laughed at something he said — genuinely laughed, not the polite kind — he was done for. That was that. Something in him had quietly rearranged itself and never gone back.

They had drifted apart the way school friendships do — gradually, then suddenly. Different high schools, different circles, different directions. But they had kept each other on Snapchat, the way you keep a photograph you don't know what to do with but can't bring yourself to throw away.

Then college happened. Sai went north for his engineering program. Zara stayed in the city and enrolled in an arts college. Their vacations never lined up. Their schedules were different planets.

But now — by some strange trick of the calendar — they were both back in the same city at the same time. Both second-year students on a break that had aligned, for once, like two gears finally catching.

They hadn't met. Not even once.

They only talked through the app. Sai told himself this was fine. He told himself there was no rush. He told himself a lot of things.

His phone buzzed at 6:58 PM. "Haha yeah, just got back from my cousin's. What's up?" He smiled before he could stop himself.

The conversation started slow and then found its rhythm. He didn't tell her about the fight. He didn't want to be that person — the one who unloads everything in the first five minutes. Instead, he just talked. About nothing. About a stupid show he'd been watching. About a meme he'd seen that reminded him of something she'd said three months ago.

She remembered. She laughed.

And slowly, without even noticing it happening, Sai felt the tightness in his chest begin to loosen. The anger from earlier didn't disappear exactly, but it moved — shifted further away, like furniture being pushed to the edges of a room so you could breathe in the middle.

After a while, he typed:

"Hey, random question — are you free tomorrow?"

"Maybe, why?"

"I was thinking we could finally actually meet? Like in person? There's that place near—"

He watched the three dots appear. Disappear.

He notice something at the corner of his eye.

Outside his window — a flash. Red. Bright and sharp, like a flare or a signal, there for a half-second and then gone. He blinked and looked harder. Just the street. A parked scooter. The orange glow of a streetlamp.

Nothing.

He shook his head slightly and looked back at his phone.

The chat had gone quiet.

He waited.

The three dots didn't come back. He waited longer. Zara has opened your snap. No reply.

Last seen — 7:23 PM.

He set the phone down. Picked it up. Checked again. The same timestamp stared back at him.

She had left the chat.

He put the phone face-down again, but this time there was no waiting, no hope behind the gesture. Just the mechanical motion of putting a thing down that you didn't know what to do with.

The room felt very quiet.

First his parents. Now this.

He didn't let himself think too much about what it meant. He was tired of thinking. He was tired of feeling things that didn't lead anywhere. He pulled the blanket over himself, still dressed, and closed his eyes.

He was asleep before he realized.

1 AM

Hunger woke him.

The sharp, inconvenient kind — the kind that doesn't negotiate. His stomach had decided that the argument and the heartache were someone else's problem, and it wanted food.

Sai sat up, groggy, and looked at his phone.

1:09 AM.

He got up, unlocked his bedroom door, and stepped into the hallway. The house was dark and still. That wasn't unusual — his parents went to bed early. What was unusual was the silence. Not the normal sleeping-house silence, but something heavier. Something that sat differently.

He walked to the kitchen and stopped.

The stove was on low heat. A pan sat on the burner, and in it were onions — half-chopped, half-fried. A cutting board on the counter held half a chopped tomato, the knife resting beside it at an angle, as if set down mid-motion. A steel bowl of beaten eggs waited nearby.

Someone had been cooking and just... stopped.

Sai turned off the stove. He stood in the kitchen for a moment, a small frown on his face

He walked to his parents' room. The door was wide open. He pushed it further and looked inside.

The bed was empty. Neatly made — his mother always made it before she slept, it was a habit she had — but empty. No one had slept in it.

Sai leaned against the doorframe and considered this.

The fight. The open door. The unfinished cooking.

They went somewhere, he decided. And because of the fight, they didn't tell me.

It stung. But it also made sense, in the way that things make sense when you're tired enough to accept the simplest explanation.

He went back to the kitchen, finished what his mother had started — beat the eggs more thoroughly, added the onions and tomato, made himself a proper omelet with salt and a little too much pepper. He ate it standing at the counter, watching the dark window above the sink, his reflection ghostly and distant in the glass.

He washed the plate. Went back to his room. Fell asleep again almost instantly.

Morning

He woke to silence.

Not unusual. He lay there for a moment, staring at the ceiling, letting himself surface. Then he turned to look out the window.

He sat up.

The tree was gone.

Not fallen. Not cut down. Gone. The large neem tree that had stood outside his window for as long as he could remember — the one that scraped the glass during monsoon, the one whose branches he had once tried and failed to climb — it was simply no longer there. In its place was only the ground, flat and bare, the earth undisturbed as though the tree had never existed.

Sai got out of bed slowly. He walked to the window and ducked his head out.

He looked left. He looked right. He looked across the street, down the lane, toward the park at the corner.

No trees. No grass. No plants of any kind. Not a single bird. Not a dog or a cat or a crow. No movement anywhere. Just buildings and roads and the pale morning sky, stripped of everything that breathed or grew.

He stayed at the window for a long time.

Then he pulled his head back inside, sat on the edge of his bed, and was very still and very quiet.

He got dressed. Laced his shoes with careful, methodical movements. Splashed water on his face and looked at himself in the mirror for exactly three seconds.

Went down, Saw nobody, Just void and silence.

He paniced. This was not a decision he made unconsciously — it was more like something in him understood, already, "The world he knew was gone"
