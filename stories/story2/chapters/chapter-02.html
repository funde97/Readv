Ultimus & the Iron Queen

Chapter Two

The Cyber Cafe

The streets were empty.

Not in the way streets are empty at five in the morning, when the world is just sleeping. Empty in the complete sense — no one sitting on steps, no tea stalls with steam rising, no rickshaws, no motorcycles. Not even the sound of a distant television leaking through a window.

Just the streets, the buildings, the sky.

And Sai, walking.

He kept his pace steady. He looked at everything but let himself react to nothing. There was a man's slipper in the middle of the road — just one, just lying there. A half-eaten banana on a bench, still yellow, freshly abandoned. A phone on the ground, screen cracked, lock screen still lit.

He did not stop. He had somewhere to be.

The cyber cafe was six blocks from his house. He'd been coming here since he was thirteen — back then, for gaming tournaments with his school friends, rounds of Counter-Strike and FIFA that stretched till the owner kicked them out. He knew the layout by heart. He knew which PCs had the best specs, which chairs weren't broken, which corner got the best signal.

The door was unlocked. Of course it was.

He stepped inside. Twenty-two PCs, all idle, their screensavers drifting lazily. The room smelled like old AC and instant noodles. He sat down at the fastest machine in the corner, the one with the most RAM.

He opened a browser.

The internet was still up. That was the first thing he needed to know — and it was true, at least for now. The pages loaded quickly. Almost suspiciously quickly, in fact. With no one else on the network, he had all of it to himself.

He cracked his knuckles and began.

He knew — with the absolute clarity of a person who has had a very good engineering education — that the servers would not last forever. Without people maintaining them, without power grids being managed, without the thousand invisible human hands that kept the internet running, it would all start dying. Maybe in days. Maybe in weeks. He couldn't know. But he was not going to waste whatever window he had.

He started with the AI models. Every open-source large language model he could find — he began downloading them all, directing the files to every PC in the room simultaneously, using a daisy-chain of external drives he found in the storage cabinet. He grabbed the medical databases next: disease guides, surgical procedure manuals, pharmaceutical references, emergency care handbooks. Then farming — soil chemistry, crop rotation, seed preservation, irrigation engineering. Energy systems: solar panel setup, wind turbine mechanics, manual generator maintenance, how to build a small hydroelectric setup from scratch.

He found a complete archive of electronics repair manuals. Then mechanical engineering textbooks — four thousand pages on machines and how to fix them. Then food preservation science — not just basic canning, but industrial-scale techniques: nitrogen sealing, freeze-drying methods, moisture control, how to store grain for a hundred years without it rotting.

He downloaded military manuals. How to strip and reassemble a rifle. Handgun maintenance. Sniper calibration. Tactical movement. Then aviation: commercial pilot training modules, helicopter basics, jet engine operation. A full submarine operations manual, dusty and classified-looking, that he found in a declassified government archive. Tank mechanics and operation. Vehicle repair across forty different engine types.

Then, because he was still human, he downloaded movies. Ten thousand of them. Every classic, every blockbuster, every beloved series he could find. Music libraries. E-books. Audiobooks. Podcasts. Comedy specials. Games — thousands of games, every genre, every size. All of it. The whole beautiful mess of human entertainment.

He is still a boy, he also downloaded somany p##ns.

He worked for eleven hours without stopping.

By the time the last drive clicked full and he leaned back in the chair, his eyes burned and his back ached and he was genuinely, deeply hungry. But the drives were stacked in three neat rows on the desk beside him, labeled in his handwriting, organized by category.

He allowed himself a small nod of satisfaction.

Then he stood up, stretched until his spine cracked in three places, and picked up the drives.

There was still a lot of work to do.

The market district was a ten-minute drive from the cyber cafe. Sai found a sturdy SUV with the keys still in the ignition — a white Toyota Land Cruiser, nearly full tank — and drove it there without hurrying.

He moved through the market the way he moved through everything now: methodically, calmly, with a clear list in his mind.

Food first. He wasn't just grabbing what he could carry — he was thinking in terms of years. He collected sacks of rice, wheat, and dried lentils by the hundreds, loading them with a hand trolley he found near the grain stalls. Sealed tins of cooking oil. Industrial-size containers of salt. Crates of sugar. Every bag of powdered milk he could find. Jars of honey, which he knew could last essentially forever. Dried beans. Rolled oats. Protein powder — entire shelves of it, every flavor and brand. He wasn't picky.

He designated a separate warehouse for each food category. The grain warehouse was a wholesale storage facility near the outer market — he broke the lock with a bolt cutter he found in a hardware store and began filling it. The dairy and powder storage went into a temperature-controlled unit nearby. He worked with a focused efficiency that surprised even himself, loading and driving and organizing in loops, until by the end of the second day the shelves were stacked ten feet high and he had enough calories to feed a small village for years.

Then electronics. He collected solar panels from every hardware and appliance store he could find, stacking them carefully. Generators — diesel and petrol. Batteries. Wiring. LED lighting systems. Oscilloscopes, soldering kits, component drawers full of resistors and capacitors. He spread the electronics storage across multiple buildings, in case of fire. Redundancy was survival.

Fuel was next, and fuel required the most care. He spent an entire day identifying a large open lot on the absolute far edge of the city — well away from any structure he planned to use — and filled it with every fuel tanker and jerry can he could source. Petrol, diesel, kerosene, LPG canisters. He mapped the lot's location on a physical notebook he kept in his chest pocket. Far. Very far. That was the whole point.

He cleared the roads. Every car, truck, or bike that sat abandoned in a lane, he moved to the side — pushing them in neutral, towing when needed. He wanted clear paths through the city. Clean escape routes. He wasn't doing it out of neatness. He was doing it because in an emergency, every second matters.

The drones came next, and this was where his AIML training finally got to do real work.

He raided three electronics shops and a university robotics lab he'd seen in passing, and over the course of a week he assembled a fleet of surveillance drones — eight of them, each equipped with cameras, proximity sensors, and the lightweight ML navigation models he'd downloaded at the cafe. He programmed them to patrol in overlapping circuits across the city, sharing data, flagging anything that moved. They could navigate themselves without him. They would be his eyes when he couldn't be everywhere at once.

For his home base, he chose a five-star hotel in the heart of the city — a tall, glass-fronted building with a backup generator system, a commercial kitchen, a gym on the twelfth floor, a rooftop garden (bare now, like everything else), and suites with floor-to-ceiling windows that let him see in every direction.

He took the penthouse suite.

He installed the generators. He ran the gym's equipment through a maintenance check. He set up his entertainment system — a wide-screen projector, surround sound, a PC rig built from the best parts he could scavenge. He chose two dozen strong vehicles from across the city and parked them in the hotel's underground garage: a military jeep, three performance bikes, a heavy truck for cargo, a black SUV with reinforced bumpers, and others. Each one fueled and ready.

He stocked the hotel's walk-in freezer with the fresh food he wanted to eat in the short term, and the pantry with everything longer-lasting. He created a weapons room on the same floor as his suite — rifles, pistols, a sniper setup, ammunition organized by caliber, everything cleaned and catalogued.

Then he sat down at the kitchen island of the penthouse, made himself a cup of black coffee, and looked out at the silent city spread below him.

It was vast and still and completely his.

He drank his coffee slowly.
