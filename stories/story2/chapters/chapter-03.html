Ultimus & the Iron Queen

Chapter Three

The Routine

The alarm was set for 6:00 AM. He never needed it more than once.

He was up before the first light, doing push-ups on the cool stone floor of the penthouse while the sky outside shifted from black to deep blue to pale gold. One hour of training — a rotation he had built himself from the military fitness manuals he'd downloaded. Calisthenics, weights, running circuits on the rooftop. He pushed himself every day. Not out of vanity — out of practicality. His body was a tool. Tools needed maintenance.

Breakfast was a serious production. High protein, every day: eggs or protein shakes, nuts, oats, fruits from the preserved stock, occasionally meat he had sealed carefully. He ate slowly and ate well, sitting by the window with the city laid out before him like a map no one else was using.

From 8 AM to noon, he studied.

He worked through the downloaded materials in a sequence he'd decided on with careful thought. Mondays were AI and systems — reinforcing what he already knew, pushing into the parts he hadn't covered yet, running models and writing code on his rig. Tuesdays were medical — anatomy, pharmacology, surgical procedures, trauma care. He was not a doctor, but he needed to know what a doctor would know. Wednesdays were engineering — electrical, mechanical, structural. How things were built and how they broke and how to fix them. Thursdays were agriculture and chemistry — soil science, plant growth, water filtration, basic compounds. Fridays were military and tactical — weapons, navigation, threat assessment, survival strategy. Weekends he followed his instincts, reading whatever he found himself most curious about.

He kept notes. Real notes, written by hand in hardcover journals he'd found in a stationery store. The act of writing it down made it stick.

Noon was lunch. Always a proper meal. Always eaten without rush.

2 PM to 4 PM — resource runs. He would drive out with a list, survey new sections of the city, collect anything useful he hadn't thought of yet, check the drone feeds, do maintenance on the vehicles. He was always finding something he hadn't considered: a hospital with an untouched surgical supply room, a university chemistry lab with materials he'd need later, a fire station with gear he added to his collection.

4 PM to 5 PM — shooting practice. He had set up a range in the hotel's long basement corridor. He worked with the pistol first: tight groupings, controlled breathing, smooth draw. Then the rifle — medium distance, consistency. Then the sniper setup on the roof, long-range targeting on distant markers he'd placed across the city. He was not naturally gifted at this. He became good through sheer repetition.

5 PM to 7 PM — video games. This was not a guilty pleasure. This was medicine. He sat in his chair, controller in hand, and let himself disappear into another world — racing games, RPGs, strategy, whatever his mood wanted. He laughed out loud sometimes, at cutscenes or absurd moments. In the silence of the hotel, his own laughter sounded strange to him at first. Then it became something he looked forward to.

Evening workout: agility and speed. Ladder drills on the rooftop, bag work, flexibility training.

Dinner was a small ritual. He cooked something real — not elaborate, but considered. A proper meal, plated like it meant something.

Then a movie. He had ten thousand to choose from and he worked through them with a loose, wandering logic — sometimes following a director's filmography, sometimes all the films of a particular decade, sometimes just picking whatever title made him feel something when he read it. He cried at some of them. Not ashamed of it. There was no one to be ashamed in front of.

And then — the diary.

Every night, before he slept, he wrote. He didn't allow himself to skip it even on the nights he didn't want to. He wrote about what he'd done that day, what he'd learned, what he'd thought about, what he'd noticed. He wrote about Zara sometimes, and his parents, and the fight that felt very far away now and also somehow like it had happened five minutes ago. He wrote about small things — a sunset that had been unexpectedly beautiful, a drone capture that caught a piece of a billboard swinging in the wind, a book he'd read that had made him rethink something he was sure of.

He was not happy, exactly.

But he was alive in a way he had never quite been before.
