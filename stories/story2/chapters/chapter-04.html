Chapter Four

The Man He Had Become

Part Three: Eighteen Months

Eighteen months.

Five hundred and forty-seven days, give or take.

The person who looked back at Sai from the hotel bathroom mirror every morning was not the same person who had locked himself in his bedroom over a fight with his parents. That boy had been lean in the soft way of someone who walked to class and occasionally played cricket. This person was different — shoulders wider, back straighter, jaw set in a way that hadn't been there before. Not the body of someone who'd been trying to look strong. The body of someone who had spent a year and a half doing hard things every single day.

The warehouse system had grown beyond what he had initially imagined. What had started as organized shelves had become a structured supply network — grain stores large enough for ten thousand people, medical supplies to run a clinic for years, fuel reserves that wouldn't run dry for decades, seeds sealed in temperature-controlled containers, a library of tools and parts and equipment that covered almost every conceivable need. He had built it without knowing what he was building it for.

Just in case.

Just because it was the right thing to do.

He tried not to think too hard about who he was building it for. That particular question had teeth.

Some evenings he sat on the hotel rooftop with a glass of water and watched the drones trace their circuits across the city, their small lights blinking in the dark like artificial fireflies. The city was clean. The roads were clear. The structures were mostly intact — nature, without its living components, had at least left the buildings alone. He had fixed what needed fixing. He had made the city functional, in the way a well-organized archive is functional — everything in its place, everything labeled, no one coming to read it.

Those evenings were the hardest.

Not because anything had gone wrong. Because everything had gone right. Because he had done everything there was to do, done it well, and the silence remained.

He never stopped checking Snapchat. The app still functioned, technically — his own phone held a local copy of the interface — but the servers had gone dark eight months ago, and there was no sending, no receiving, nothing. Just the frozen last state of every conversation. Zara's name at the top of his chat list. Her last message, unanswered.

"Maybe, why?"

He had never finished typing his answer.

He closed the app and didn't open it again.

Chapter 10 — The Ferris Wheel

It was a Tuesday when he went to the amusement park. He often went there on Tuesdays. He wasn't sure when it had become a habit — somewhere around month seven, when the restlessness had started finding different ways to show itself, he had begun doing things simply because they were somewhere to go.

The park was in the middle of the city — a sprawling complex of rides and game stalls and food courts, all of it motionless and overgrown in the non-living sense: covered in dust, faded by months of direct sun, the painted cartoon faces on the walls looking mildly unsettling without the noise and movement of people to contextualize them.

He liked it here. He wasn't entirely sure why. Maybe because it was a place that had been built entirely for joy, and there was something both sad and comforting about that.

He rode the carousel sometimes — turned it on, sat on one of the horses, let it spin. Watched the world go in circles. It was a strange feeling and he chased it anyway.

But today he walked past the carousel without stopping. Past the game stalls, past the bumper cars sitting still and waiting, past the cotton candy machine with its cracked dome. He walked until he stood in front of the Ferris wheel.

It was the largest structure in the park — maybe sixty feet high at the apex, a great steel circle with twelve gondola compartments painted alternating blue and yellow. It hadn't been running in months. He looked at it for a long moment.

Then he got in.

He found the manual control panel in the operator's booth, bypassed the safety lockout the way he'd learned to bypass a dozen similar systems, and started the wheel moving. It groaned at first — the groan of machinery waking from long sleep — then found its rhythm and began to turn, slow and steady, carrying him upward.

He watched the park fall away below him. Then the park's edges. Then the streets, spreading out in all directions, the city's silent geometry made visible. He could see the hotel from here — a glint of glass in the middle distance. He could see the fuel depot at the far edge, barely a smudge on the horizon.

He could see everything. And everything was empty.

When the gondola reached the top, he did something he hadn't planned to do.

He pulled out his pistol and shot the wheel's control panel in the operator's booth below.

The wheel shuddered and stopped.

He sat for a moment in the swinging gondola. Then he opened the door, stepped out onto the narrow maintenance ledge outside it, grabbed the steel frame of the wheel, and began to climb.

It wasn't technically difficult. He had the grip strength and the fearlessness that comes from not particularly caring whether something goes wrong. The steel was warm from the afternoon sun. He climbed steadily, hand over hand, foot finding each crossbar with the confidence of someone who had spent eighteen months doing harder things than this.

He reached the top.

He stood on the highest crossbar of the Ferris wheel — sixty feet above the city — and looked out.

The wind was light up here. It moved through his hair and over his face in a way that felt almost kind. The sky was a clear, saturated blue, going orange at the western edges. The city spread in every direction: buildings and roads and the geometry of a million human decisions, all of them frozen, all of them still, all of them his in the most hollow sense of ownership.

He had collected enough to sustain ten thousand people.

There were no people.

He had built a life that was, by any reasonable measure, extraordinary.

There was no one to share it with.

He had learned medicine, engineering, farming, combat, cooking, music theory, film history, five programming languages he hadn't known before, the taxonomic classification of crops, and how to read the stars.

There was no one to tell.

He stood at the top of the Ferris wheel for a long time, the city below him and the sky above him, and for the first time in eighteen months, he let himself sit with the thing he had been outrunning.

He was alone.

Completely. Totally. Permanently, maybe.

He had been so busy. The routine was genius in that way — genius and cowardly, both. If you filled every hour, you didn't have to sit in the space between them. If you were always building, always learning, always moving, you didn't have to ask what any of it was for.

He looked down.

Sixty feet. The ground was a long way down but not unimaginably so. He had studied enough physics to do the math without wanting to.

His toes moved slightly forward on the steel bar.

The wind moved through his hair again.

He closed his eyes.

And then —

Red.

He opened his eyes.

There it was. The same flash he had seen eighteen months ago, through his bedroom window, in the half-second before Zara had left the chat. Red light, sharp and strange, appearing and holding and pulsing — not like a fire, not like a traffic light, not like anything he could name.

It was coming from somewhere beyond the buildings. Four, maybe five roads ahead, in the direction of the old market district. He couldn't see the source. The buildings blocked it. But he could see the light itself climbing up into the sky, staining the low clouds above that section of the city a deep and unmistakable red.

He stood very still on top of the Ferris wheel.

His toes moved back from the edge.

He stared at the light.

The light stared back.
